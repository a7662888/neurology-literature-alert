## PubMed Abstract

The risk of developing dementia at any given age has fallen steadily in Europe and the United States over recent decades. A key unresolved question is whether recent generations reach older age with better cognitive capacity, experience slower cognitive decline, or both, as this has implications for when preventive interventions are likely to have the greatest impact. Analyzing ~783,000 episodic-memory assessments from ~219,000 individuals in five longitudinal studies followed for up to 30 years, we find that later-born generations benefit from a double dividend, with higher memory levels entering old age and slower decline thereafter. The combined advantage is large enough to plausibly account for the reported 13% per-decade decline in dementia incidence. Improvements are concentrated among the fastest decliners; later-born generations show longer periods of stable memory, and their slower decline is mirrored by lower rates of clinical impairment and slower hippocampal atrophy. Calendar-time trends affecting all generations simultaneously are unlikely to explain these differences. Because memory level is built mainly in early life whereas the rate of decline is shaped by conditions in adulthood, sustaining the decline in dementia incidence will require prevention policies that protect brain health across the whole lifespan, beginning in childhood.
