## PubMed Abstract

INTRODUCTION: Differentiating aspects of sleep health that are modifiable risk factors from prodromal symptoms or consequences of dementia can inform dementia prevention and/or clinical screening efforts, and temporal mapping of sleep-cognition associations can further inform mechanistic hypotheses regarding the role of sleep leading up to dementia diagnosis. We examined bidirectional associations between cognitive function and three modifiable sleep health features, and whether these associations vary by proximity to dementia diagnosis.

METHODS: 291 preclinical dementia participants (median initial age = 83 years, 73% female) and 281 matched cognitively unimpaired 'controls' from the Rush Memory and Aging Project completed up to 11 annual assessments of global cognition (composite of 19 neuropsychological measures) and actigraphy-derived measures of sleep amount, regularity, and timing. Linear mixed-effects models tested whether bidirectional sleep-cognition relationships differed by years-to-dementia diagnosis (preclinical dementia sample) or years-to-last follow-up (matched controls) after adjusting for sociodemographic and health covariates.

RESULTS: In the preclinical dementia sample, time-to-dementia modified the effects of sleep amount (both β = 0.02) and regularity (-0.04 < β < 0.02), but not timing, on next-year global cognition. Ten years before dementia diagnosis, longer and more irregular sleep predicted better next-year cognition; 1 year before diagnosis, these sleep patterns predicted worse next-year cognition. While sleep amount unidirectionally predicted subsequent cognition, sleep regularity-cognition associations were consistently bidirectional. These patterns were not observed in matched controls.

DISCUSSION: Changes in sleep-cognition associations over time may differentiate preclinical dementia from normal aging. Future research should investigate the driving mechanisms and modifiability of these sleep-cognition associations.
