## PubMed Abstract

Stroke causes severe motor deficits, but functional recovery can occur through neural circuit reorganization in areas adjacent to and remote from the infarct. Optogenetic stimulation can modulate neuronal activity and improve recovery in experimental models, yet the population-level cortical dynamics underlying these effects remain poorly understood. The lateral cerebellar nucleus projects to the cortex via the dentatothalamocortical pathway and represents a promising target for remote neuromodulation after stroke. We performed longitudinal one-photon calcium imaging of ipsilesional primary motor cortex layer V neurons in awake, behaving mice (n = 13) following transient middle cerebral artery occlusion. Mice received optogenetic stimulation of the contralesional lateral cerebellar nucleus using CaMKII-driven Channelrhodopsin-2 on post-stroke days 5 through 14. Stimulation-treated and non-stimulated stroke mice were compared across behavioral and neural activity measures for up to 28 days post-stroke. Stroke significantly reduced ipsilesional motor cortex population activity during the subacute phase, with decreased calcium peak frequency (p < 0.001) and amplitude (p = 0.004). Reduced frequency was strongly associated with lesion size at post-stroke day 6 (r² = 0.88, p < 0.001), and reduced amplitude was similarly associated with lesion size at post-stroke day 9 (r² = 0.39, p = 0.022). Cerebellar stimulation counteracted these deficits, significantly increasing both frequency (p = 0.017) and amplitude (p < 0.001) and improving behavioral recovery on the rotating beam task (p = 0.027). Critically, in stimulated animals, motor cortex activity was no longer coupled to lesion size, indicating that stimulation decoupled cortical dynamics from injury severity. Early post-stroke functional connection density among motor cortex neurons predicted long-term behavioral outcome in non-stimulated animals (r² = 0.50, p = 0.033), but this relationship was abolished by stimulation, suggesting that cerebellar stimulation reshaped the recovery trajectory independently of initial network disruption. Stroke also shifted neuronal populations toward minimally active states, and stimulation preserved a higher proportion of moderately active neurons (p = 0.029). These findings define population-level changes in peri-lesional motor cortex after stroke at cellular resolution and demonstrate that cerebellar optogenetic stimulation counteracts stroke-induced cortical dysfunction, decouples recovery from lesion burden, and promotes functional recovery.
