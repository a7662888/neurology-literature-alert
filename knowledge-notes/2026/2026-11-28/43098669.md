## PubMed Abstract

Progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) is a rare, rapidly progressive neurodegenerative parkinsonian disorder and primary four-repeat tauopathy for which no disease-modifying therapies currently exist. Although tau pathology is central to PSP, additional mechanisms are likely to contribute to disease onset and progression. Among these, mitochondrial dysfunction has emerged as a biologically plausible but incompletely defined component of PSP pathophysiology. In this review, we summarize current evidence linking mitochondrial impairment to PSP across human tissue studies, cellular models, and omics-based analyses. Available data indicate quantitative and/or functional abnormalities in the mitochondrial respiratory chain, impaired bioenergetics, and increased oxidative stress. Omics-based studies further suggest possible involvement of additional respiratory chain components, including complex V, although these findings may be influenced by altered cell and tissue composition. Emerging cell-type-resolved studies further indicate that these mitochondrial changes may be highly cell-type-specific in the PSP brain. However, current evidence remains limited and often inconsistent across models and human tissues. Key unanswered questions include the type, neuroanatomical extent, and cell-type distribution of mitochondrial respiratory chain abnormalities in the central nervous system and peripheral tissues, as well as the upstream and downstream mechanistic significance of these changes in PSP pathogenesis, including their relationship to tau pathology. Addressing these questions will require more direct studies in human tissues using cell-type-resolved and spatially informed approaches. Such efforts will be essential for defining the pathogenic relevance of mitochondrial dysfunction in PSP and for evaluating its potential as a therapeutic target. © 2026 International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society.
